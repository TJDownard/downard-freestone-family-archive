A note on the evidence

History, with the records showing.

This narrative combines the family relationships and dates supplied for this archive with published historical research and institutional records. Contemporary records, later scholarly reconstructions, family traditions, and unresolved claims are distinguished throughout. A date in a family tree is not automatically a verified historical fact.

Chapter 01

An ocean between two lives.

In 1635, Barnabas Lamson left England for Massachusetts Bay. His family’s history would become part of the first generation of colonial Cambridge.

The family’s English home was associated with Ridgewell, Essex. Parish entries cited by genealogists record the baptism of Barnabas’s son Barnabas on 22 November 1631 and daughter Martha on 13 January 1633/34. The second explicitly names Barnabas and Marie as parents. These are more substantial clues than an unsourced place of birth.

The 1908 Lamson genealogy reports that Barnabas sailed from Harwich on the Defence on 10 August 1635, alongside the party of the minister Thomas Shepard. The more recent Great Migration study places his immigration in 1635 and his first residence in Cambridge. The specific sailing date and his appearance on a surviving passenger roll have not been independently verified here; the surviving online passenger-list transcription should be checked against the claim.

What was the voyage like? Atlantic crossings in the seventeenth century were difficult, with cramped quarters, uncertain weather, and weeks at sea. Those are general conditions, not a recovered diary of Barnabas’s particular journey. No personal shipboard account has been located.

Chapter 02

Two houses in a new town.

Within months of arrival, Barnabas was involved in the practical work of building and governing Cambridge.

On 23 November 1635, Cambridge appointed Barnabas highway surveyor; on 7 November 1636, he became a selectman, one of the men entrusted with the town’s business. These are identifiable civic offices—not a family legend. The town was still known in its early years as New Towne.

A February 1635/36 list credited Barnabas with two houses in Cambridge. He held six acres in the upper division and six in the lower division on the south side of the Charles River. By 1639, recorded transactions named Nathaniel Sparhawk, Gregory Stone, and John Cooper as buyers of portions of his property. The family history also associates his land with the site later occupied by the Oliver Wendell House, but that location should be mapped from original Cambridge deeds before assigning a modern street address.

One 1636 town entry refers to an agreement involving “Goodman Lamson” and a boy tending cattle across the river. The passage suggests a working agricultural household, although it does not establish Barnabas’s principal occupation. He was a landholder and town official; his exact trade remains unknown.

His story has an address in the historical record—even if we cannot yet translate that address into a modern house number.

Chapter 03

Five children, five guardians.

Barnabas and Marie both died within a few years of the family’s arrival. Their children remained in the historical record.

Marie’s family-tree entry gives a death on 17 March 1638, while Barnabas died around 1640. His oral, or nuncupative, will directed that the five children be placed with named men: Mary with Sparhawk; Sarah with Isaac; Barnabas Jr. with Parish; Martha with Stone; Joseph with Bridge. The precise relationships of these guardians to the family still require investigation.

For Joseph, the direct ancestral link is particularly strong. A January 1658/59 Cambridge church account names Joseph as Barnabas’s son and records him in the care of John Bridge. The same scholarly account connects Joseph to Elizabeth Rice, whom he married around 1661 according to that reconstruction (family-tree entries instead say 1663).

A later will by Boston merchant Peter Lidget names women associated with the Crouch, Cook, and Rice families—the married surnames of three Lamson daughters. It offers a valuable clue to Marie’s possible Liggett/Lidget family, but it does not by itself prove that her father was Thomas Liggett.

Important distinction: Marie’s exact death date and Thomas Liggett’s parentage are family-tree claims awaiting original records. The guardianship arrangements and Joseph’s identification as Barnabas’s son are supported by historical records summarized in The Great Migration.
Chapter 04

The Revolution reaches Connecticut.

Elnathan Lambson lived through the American Revolution. His connection to the militia needs careful labeling.

Elnathan Lambson (1733–1827), grandson of Joseph Lamson Jr., was born in colonial Simsbury and lived into the early nineteenth century. A genealogical reference points to a 1779 alarm-list or militia readiness record involving Captain Bates and Colonel Phelps’s Connecticut regiment. An alarm list identified men expected to equip themselves and respond if summoned.

That is not equivalent to proof of marching, battlefield action, Continental Army enlistment, or veteran status. The underlying roll must be inspected to confirm that the Elnathan named is this ancestor. For now his biography carries a militia research lead, not a verified veteran badge.

Jesse Lambson Sr. (1756–1818) has a reported 1780 military registration; again, registration is not proof of service. Later, Arba Lorenzo Lambson Sr. (1818–1870/71) has a reported 1866 registration, and Glenn Cody Lambson (1898–1934) a reported 1918 World War I draft registration. Neither is evidence of deployment or active service.

Military verification standard: a dated original roster, service record, pension file, or muster roll naming the correct person is required before using “veteran” or a military-service insignia. A wagon-company captain (below) was a civilian migration leader, not a military rank.
Chapter 05

A captain on the overland road.

One of the strongest new discoveries is not military at all: Arba Lorenzo Lambson Sr. led pioneer wagon companies.

Arba Lorenzo Lambson Sr., born in New York in 1818, moved through the American Midwest before reaching Utah. The Church History Biographical Database identifies him as the captain of an Arba L. Lambson wagon company in 1861, organized at Florence, Nebraska, and traveling toward Salt Lake City. It also identifies his appointment as captain of a second company on 23 July 1862.

The 1861 record lists his traveling group, including wife Sarah Cordellia Harrington and children, among them the infant Arba Lorenzo Lambson Jr., the next direct ancestor. This is a remarkably specific link between your family and the overland migration.

His role matters: a wagon captain coordinated people, equipment, travel, and decisions across a demanding route. It should be featured prominently in the story and his biography—while clearly distinguished from military command.

The parallel with the Downard family is striking. George Downard crossed the plains in the Horton D. Haight company in 1862. The BYU study of Latter-day Saint emigration lists the Arba L. Lambson company departing July 23 and the Haight company departing August 10 that same year. Two branches of the later family were on the overland route in the same summer.

Chapter 06

Work, homes, and the Utah years.

The later Lambsons were not just names on a migration list. They worked, married, raised children, and made lives in Utah.

The family-tree account places Arba Sr. in Springville, Utah, and associates him with blacksmithing. An 1867 directory lists “Lambson A. L.” on Main Street, Springville under boot and shoe makers, while “Lambson A. B.” appears under blacksmiths. This is an unusually valuable address-level lead—but initials alone cannot establish which individual owned the shop. It may correct an inherited assumption about Arba’s occupation.

The Wilford Woodruff Papers gives Arba Sr.’s death as 1 November 1870 in Springville; the Church History Biographical Database instead records 1 November 1871. The date conflict should remain visible until reconciled against burial, probate, and newspaper records.

His son Arba Lorenzo Lambson Jr. (1860–1910) and wife Rosella Francenia Blanchard (1865–1909) raised a large family in Springville. Their son Glenn Cody Lambson (1898–1934) later lived in Bingham Canyon, a Utah mining community. Glenn married Mary Lucille Jamieson (1905–1984) in Salt Lake City in 1922. The precise family home or employment address in Bingham Canyon has not yet been located.

Address research: 1867 Utah directory (Springville, Main Street) ↗; Wilford Woodruff Papers profile ↗. “Main Street” is a historical business location, not a verified modern street number or residential homestead.
Chapter 07

Two histories become one.

In 1942, the old colonial New England line met the Downard family’s more recent English immigration story.

Norma Lucille Lambson was born 18 April 1923 in Bingham Canyon, Utah. Her father Glenn died when she was eleven. She married Thomas John Downard in Salt Lake City on 26 June 1942.

Thomas, born in Bluebell in 1921, served in the U.S. Army during World War II according to family information, with a reported assignment to the 136th Infantry Regiment, 33rd Infantry Division. That unit attribution should be checked against a discharge or personnel record before it is described as independently verified.

Their children included Robert T., Glen L., and Quinn C. Through Robert T. Downard, the Lambson and Downard lines lead into the modern family. Barnabas’s reported 1635 sea crossing and George Downard’s documented 1862 migration had become chapters of one family history.

Different oceans, different centuries, one family.
Family voices · Clara Lambson Miller

The life behind the dates.

Clara Lambson Miller (1888–1981), a daughter of Arba Lorenzo Lambson Jr. and Rosella Blanchard, left a remarkable autobiographical account of the household in which Glenn Cody Lambson grew up.

Clara recalled her grandfather Arba Lorenzo Lambson Sr. and his wife Sarah Harrington bringing an organ and sewing machine into the Salt Lake Valley. She described their Springville home as having a rear lean-to and root cellar, with a large four-poster bed and a canopy. These are Clara’s family recollections, not independently verified inventories.

Her memories of childhood are wonderfully specific: kerosene lamps cleaned and refilled, old clothing cut into strips for braided rugs, straw laid under carpeting, milk churned into butter, and eggs and butter carried to a local store in exchange for supplies. Apples were dried and stored for winter; a copper kettle was used for soapmaking. Family evenings meant a warm fire, preserved fruit, and her father reading the newspaper aloud.

She also remembered her mother Rosella making the home attractive with wallpaper and paint. The children included Minnie, Zella, Ernest, Arch, and Glenn. Clara described her father building a brick house on land acquired from her mother’s family, and remembered the Blanchard and Lambson households in Springville.

A family history becomes something different when someone tells us what was on the stove, how the rooms were warmed, and who read aloud by the fire.

Clara also recorded stories of tense encounters between early settlers and Indigenous people. Those passages are inherited frontier memories, told from one family’s perspective; they should not be treated as a balanced or independently verified account of Native communities.

Source: Clara Lambson Miller, Autobiography, pp. 1–4, photographed in the FamilySearch Memories screenshots supplied for this archive. Clara explicitly distinguishes events she remembered from stories told by her grandparents.
A newly surfaced family testimony

Glenn and the Great War.

Clara’s autobiography contains a direct family statement about her brother Glenn Cody Lambson that deserves follow-up in military archives.

In her discussion of the Service Star Legion, Clara wrote that while the family lived in Bingham, her brother Glenn Lambson “went to War in France.” This is stronger evidence than a draft-registration card alone: it is a sister’s retrospective account of wartime service overseas. It is not, however, a substitute for an enlistment record, unit roster, transport manifest, or discharge document.

Clara joined the Service Star Legion in 1929 and later held state and national leadership roles, including national president in 1953. Her extensive work supporting veterans gives the family story a second twentieth-century theme: the impact of war on the households that remained at home.

Research status: Glenn’s service in France is reported by his sister Clara; branch, unit, exact dates, and deployment are not independently confirmed. His profile should now say “World War I overseas service reported in Clara Lambson Miller’s autobiography,” rather than simply “draft registration only.”
Source: Clara Lambson Miller, Autobiography, p. 6, section “Service Star Legion,” supplied FamilySearch screenshots.
Research finding · conflicting dates

When two records disagree.

The Wilford Woodruff Papers biographical entry gives Arba Lorenzo Lambson Sr.’s death as 1 November 1870 in Springville. Its notes cite a Utah probate file, no. 362, dated 1 November 1870, and a contemporary church-membership record. The Church History Biographical Database, however, gives 1 November 1871. The date is therefore presented as 1870/71 until the underlying probate and burial records are examined.

Institutional wagon-company records independently identify him as captain of the 1861 and 1862 companies. His 1861 traveling group included Sarah Harrington and infant Arba Jr. That is a documented leadership role, not simply a tradition of having crossed the plains.

Next primary-source checks: Utah County probate file 362; the 1861/62 company source materials (including the 1923 Beaver Press article); a verified military file for Glenn; and a full scan of Clara’s autobiography to capture the remaining pages accurately.
